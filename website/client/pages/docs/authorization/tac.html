Authorization

Default deny over roles, grants, groups, and bounded conditions. Every answer carries a stable reason and the policy version it was decided under, so an audit can say why and not just no.

Asking a question

A decision names the tenant, the principal, the action, and the resource. Nothing is implied: with no matching grant, the answer is deny with deny_no_grant.

Actions and resources are opaque strings that you choose. The convention in the examples — doc:read on project:alpha — is only a convention; SESAME never parses them for meaning, which is what keeps your model out of its vocabulary.

Granting

Create an immutable role, then grant it. A role's permissions are action–resource pairs, and either side may be the wildcard *. 

# A role is immutable: changing what it means in place would silently
# rewrite the meaning of every grant pointing at it.
role = client.role_create(tenant_id, "editor", [
    {"action": "doc:read", "resource": "project:*"},
    {"action": "doc:write", "resource": "project:alpha"},
])

grant = client.grant_create(tenant_id, principal_id, role["role_id"])

# Revoking is immediate and durable; the next decision reflects it.
client.grant_revoke(grant["grant_id"])

A wildcard matches one segment or the whole value, and it never matches across a boundary you did not write — project:* covers project:alpha, not projectile.

Groups

Grant a role to a group and every member inherits it. Membership is resolved at decision time, so adding someone to a group takes effect on their next request rather than their next login. 

group = client.group_create(tenant_id, "engineering")
client.group_member_add(group["group_id"], principal_id)

# Grants attach to the group exactly as they attach to a principal.
client.grant_create(tenant_id, group["group_id"], role["role_id"])

# An allow through a group is reported as such, not as a role grant.
# {"decision": "allow", "reason_code": "allow_group_grant", ...}

The reason distinguishes allow_role_grant from allow_group_grant deliberately: when you are working out why someone has access they should not have, the difference between a direct grant and an inherited one is the whole question.

Conditions

A permission may carry equality conditions on context. The caller supplies context with the request; every condition must match for the permission to apply. 

role = client.role_create(tenant_id, "regional-editor", [{
    "action": "doc:write",
    "resource": "project:*",
    "conditions": {"region": "eu"},
}])

decision = client.decide({
    "tenant_id": tenant_id, "principal_id": principal_id,
    "action": "doc:write", "resource": "project:alpha",
    "context": {"region": "eu"},
})

Two refusals are worth telling apart. A supplied value that does not match denies with deny_no_grant — from the caller's side that is simply no applicable grant. An attribute that was never supplied denies with deny_missing_context, and that code names the missing attribute only when every value you did supply already matched. Naming it otherwise would be false advice: it would suggest adding a key that still would not grant access.

Trusted context, and why you cannot supply it

Some context is too important to accept from the caller. Pass a session instead of a principal and the engine verifies it and derives context itself under the reserved session. prefix — currently session.assurance.

A caller-supplied key beginning with session. is rejected, not ignored. That refusal is the whole reason the condition is worth trusting: if the engine quietly dropped such a key, a caller could not tell whether its own value had been used. An unusable session denies with deny_session_invalid.

All ten SDKs can express this. Python, Node.js, and Go take a request object, so a session is just another key; the other seven carry a named decideForSession beside their positional decide. A contract test asserts every shim can reach the capability, because a shim that cannot is the same kind of drift as a missing operation.

Batches and repeatability

Rendering a page usually means asking many questions at once. A batch answers them under a single policy version, so a policy change mid-render cannot produce a half-old, half-new page. 

decisions = client.decide_batch([
    {"tenant_id": t, "principal_id": p, "action": "doc:read", "resource": "project:alpha"},
    {"tenant_id": t, "principal_id": p, "action": "doc:write", "resource": "project:alpha"},
])

# Pin an earlier version to reproduce a past answer exactly.
replay = client.decide(request, policy_version=4)

Where to enforce

SESAME decides; your application enforces. A decision is advice until your code acts on it, and the engine cannot stop a handler that never asked. Put the call at the boundary that owns the resource — not in a UI layer, which only controls what is offered rather than what is reachable.
